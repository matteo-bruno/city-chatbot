# The 15-minute city: concept and debate

## Definition

The **15-minute city** is an urban planning model in which residents can reach
the essential services and amenities of daily life within a 15-minute walk or
bicycle ride from home. It was popularised by Carlos Moreno and colleagues
(Moreno et al., *Smart Cities*, 2021) and gained wide attention during and
after the COVID-19 pandemic, when local neighbourhoods regained importance.

The idea is much older than the name. Clarence Perry's **neighbourhood unit**
(1929) already organised city districts around walkable access to schools and
shops. Related formulations include the *x-minute city* (10, 20, 30 minutes),
the *20-minute neighbourhood*, *chrono-urbanism* and *proximity-based city*.

## What it is meant to achieve

- Cut car dependency, and with it transport CO2, congestion and air pollution.
- Give back time otherwise lost to long-distance commuting.
- Strengthen local communities and local economies.
- Reduce social disparities in access to opportunity.

## Standing criticisms and caveats

These matter when answering "is X a 15-minute city?", because the honest answer
is always partly about the limits of the metric.

- **Time is not quality.** Two areas can both be perfectly "15-minute" while
  one has top-tier services and the other inferior ones. Proximity alone can
  therefore coexist with inequality and segregation.
- **It is not universally feasible.** In low-density, sprawling cities the
  number of amenities per resident needed to reach 15-minute access becomes
  economically unsustainable. The model is not a one-size-fits-all solution.
- **Not all services can be replicated.** A grocery store can open anywhere; a
  hospital, an opera house, a university or a stadium cannot be duplicated in
  every neighbourhood. Treating all categories identically is a simplification.
- **Densification has costs.** Pushing compactness can reduce green space.
- **Proximity is not a substitute for transit.** Fast, reliable public
  transport is still needed, especially to serve low-density peripheries.
- **Time-based versus value-based.** A richer framing optimises the
  *opportunities* available to residents, not only the transit time to them.
- **It is not a restriction on movement.** The model describes access, not
  permission to travel. Conspiracy framings that describe it as confinement
  have no basis in the research literature.

## Related terms

- **Proximity time (PT)** - the accessibility score defined below; the number
  this chatbot's data actually contains.
- **F15** - the percentage of a city's residents living where PT is at most
  15 minutes.
- **POI (point of interest)** - an amenity or service, sourced from
  OpenStreetMap: a shop, school, park, clinic, transit stop, and so on.
- **15-minute neighbourhood** - an area, rather than a whole city, whose
  proximity time is at most 15 minutes.
